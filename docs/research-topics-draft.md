# Research topics draft

Prepared 2026-10-10. “Verified by opening” means the linked page or document was opened during preparation; the brief still needs a human-quality synthesis.

## 1. Can the U.S. grid absorb data-center growth?

Find out how quickly U.S. data-center electricity use is growing, which regions face the sharpest load pressure, and whether the likely near-term answer is new generation, transmission, flexible demand, or some combination. I want a grounded comparison of official forecasts with a transparent back-of-the-envelope calculation.

Subquestions: What was data-center use in 2014 and 2023? What range does DOE project for 2028? Which regions does EIA identify as fastest-growing? How much faster is the high-demand scenario than the baseline? What generation source supplies the modeled increment? What price effect is modeled for ERCOT and PJM?

Reference points:

- DOE/LBNL report summary: 58 TWh in 2014, 176 TWh in 2023, and 325–580 TWh projected for 2028; **verified by opening** ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-releases-new-report-evaluating-increase-electricity-demand-data-centers)).
- EIA’s March 2026 analysis: demand grew 1.7% annually in 2020–25 versus 0.1% in 2005–19; **verified by opening** ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php/detail.php?id=67344)).
- EIA models ERCOT 2027 wholesale prices $37/MWh above its baseline in the high-demand scenario; **verified by opening** ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php/detail.php?id=67344)).

Hard part: projections, regional definitions, and scenario assumptions must not be mixed as if they were observations; at least one calculation is required.

Who can grade it: power-systems analyst or utility economist.

## 2. What happens to the Colorado River after 2026?

Find out what operating rules actually govern the Colorado River in 2026, what changes are being negotiated for 2027 onward, and how the alternatives distribute shortage risk. The report should separate binding rules, proposed frameworks, and political claims, then calculate what a stated million-acre-foot change means relative to Lower Basin deliveries.

Subquestions: Which agreements are incorporated into the 2026 AOP? What reservoir condition and shortage status does the AOP assume? Which rules expire at the end of 2026? What alternatives are under consideration? Which parties disagree, and over what? How large is any proposed delivery change compared with the AOP baseline?

Reference points:

- The 2026 AOP says it is separate from Post-2026 negotiations and incorporates the interim shortage and drought-contingency arrangements; **verified by opening** ([Bureau of Reclamation AOP 2026](https://www.usbr.gov/lc/region/g4000/aop/AOP26.pdf)).
- Reclamation’s Post-2026 page lists the Record of Decision, 2027–28 guidelines, and 2027–36 framework; **verified by opening** ([Reclamation Post-2026](https://www.usbr.gov/ColoradoRiverBasin/post2026/)).
- The AOP is an official primary document that can anchor the delivery and elevation calculations; **verified by opening** ([AOP PDF](https://www.usbr.gov/lc/region/g4000/aop/AOP26.pdf)).

Hard part: the legal status and time horizon of each document differ, and state positions are not interchangeable with adopted rules.

Who can grade it: Western-water lawyer, river hydrologist, or Reclamation analyst.

## 3. Why do 2025 Arctic sea-ice numbers differ?

Find out what the leading official datasets say about the 2025 Arctic sea-ice maximum and minimum, why their dates and areas differ, and which conclusions survive the methodological differences. The key task is to compare like with like and calculate the disagreement as both an area and a percentage.

Subquestions: What did NSIDC report for the 2025 maximum and minimum? What did USNIC report? Are the observation dates the same? What averaging windows and spatial resolutions differ? What is the long-run trend in the NOAA series? Does the disagreement alter the ranking or only the exact value?

Reference points:

- NOAA Arctic Report Card 2025 gives March and September monthly averages and long-run trends; **verified by opening** ([NOAA sea ice 2025](https://arctic.noaa.gov/report-card/report-card-2025/sea-ice-2025/)).
- USNIC and NSIDC were identified as using different minimum summaries in the official comparison release; the page was not readable in the browser, so **not opened** ([USNIC comparison](https://usnicweb2.espc.nesdis.noaa.gov/PressRelease/ArcticMinimumMaximum2025_2026)).
- NOAA’s broader 2025 report supplies the climate context; **verified by opening** ([NOAA Arctic Report Card](https://arctic.noaa.gov/report-card/report-card-2025/)).

Hard part: a small model may mistake different metrics and dates for a substantive scientific contradiction.

Who can grade it: cryosphere scientist or remote-sensing analyst.

## 4. What is the current U.S. PFAS drinking-water rule?

Find out what the federal PFAS drinking-water standard requires, what changed in 2026, which compounds remain covered, and what implementation questions are still unresolved. Keep this as regulatory analysis—not health advice—and distinguish a final rule from a proposal or guidance document.

Subquestions: Which compounds and limits did the 2024 rule establish? What monitoring, notification, and treatment obligations follow? What did EPA propose in May 2026? Which compounds might be removed or delayed? How do federal requirements interact with state rules? What evidence would confirm a final change?

Reference points:

- EPA’s 2024 announcement describes the first nationwide enforceable PFAS drinking-water standard and its six-PFAS scope; **not opened in this pass** ([EPA 2024 announcement](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/biden-harris-administration-finalizes-first-ever-national-drinking-water-standard-to-protect-100M-people-from-pfas-pollution)).
- EPA’s current PFAS page describes the May 18, 2026 proposed changes, including retaining PFOA/PFOS and reconsidering other compounds; **verified by opening** ([EPA PFAS page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas?_sft_rtype=case-studies&sf_paged=4)).
- EPA’s supporting-materials page is the natural place to check the rule text and technical record; **not opened in this pass** ([EPA supporting materials](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/additional-supporting-materials-final-pfas-npdwr)).

Hard part: legal status, compliance dates, and scientific risk language are easy to collapse into an inaccurate single claim.

Who can grade it: environmental regulatory attorney or drinking-water engineer.

## 5. Which cement decarbonization levers matter most?

Find out how the cement and concrete sector can reduce emissions, which measures are available now, and why the cost and maturity of those measures remain contested. Build a simple contribution calculation from an official pathway’s percentages or intensities, while clearly labeling assumptions.

Subquestions: What are the main sources of cement emissions? What do material efficiency, alternative fuels, supplementary cementitious materials, and CCS each address? Which measures are available before 2030? What does IEA say about near-zero CCS plant cost? Where do technology claims disagree? How should cement and concrete be analyzed separately?

Reference points:

- IEA says total cement/concrete emissions are higher today than in 2015 while direct emissions intensity is unchanged; **verified by opening** ([IEA Breakthrough Agenda 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/breakthrough-agenda-report-2025/cement-and-concrete)).
- IEA identifies material efficiency, alternative fuels, SCMs, and CCS as the main pathway families; **verified by opening** ([IEA cement and concrete](https://www.iea.org/reports/breakthrough-agenda-report-2025/cement-and-concrete)).
- IEA gives a 75–150% higher cost range for near-zero-emissions plants using CCS; **verified by opening** ([IEA cement and concrete](https://www.iea.org/reports/breakthrough-agenda-report-2025/cement-and-concrete)).

Hard part: process emissions, fuel emissions, concrete use, and plant-level costs are different accounting layers.

Who can grade it: cement-process engineer or industrial decarbonization researcher.

## 6. Is the Hubble tension a measurement problem or new physics?

Find out why local measurements of the Hubble constant remain higher than early-universe inferences, what independent checks have been attempted, and what uncertainty would have to disappear before “new physics” became the better explanation. Include a calculation that makes the percentage gap explicit.

Subquestions: What values do the local distance ladder and CMB inference give? What are the main steps in the ladder? What did Webb confirm? How large is the numerical gap? Which alternative distance indicators exist? What observations would discriminate calibration error from a cosmological-model problem?

Reference points:

- NASA summarizes local values around 70–76 versus CMB values around 67–68 km/s/Mpc; **verified by opening** ([NASA Hubble tension](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-behind-the-discoveries/hubble-constant-and-tension/)).
- NASA reports that Webb and Hubble agreement makes a simple Hubble measurement error less likely; **not opened in this pass** ([NASA Webb/Hubble](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/nasas-webb-hubble-telescopes-affirm-universes-expansion-rate-puzzle-persists/)).
- NASA’s page gives the standard cosmological context and age estimate near 13.8 billion years; **verified by opening** ([NASA Hubble tension](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-behind-the-discoveries/hubble-constant-and-tension/)).

Hard part: the same word “measurement” covers a ladder of calibrations, model assumptions, and correlated uncertainties.

Who can grade it: observational cosmologist.

## 7. What is happening to U.S. housing affordability?

Find out whether the recent movement in the national housing-affordability index reflects mortgage rates, prices, incomes, or inventory, and how that national signal compares with vacancy data. The report should calculate a small index change and explain why affordability and vacancy are not the same measure.

Subquestions: What were the latest fixed-index values? What was the recent high and low? How much did the index change? What do Census vacancy and homeownership tables measure? Why can a market have high affordability but low vacancy, or vice versa? Which parts vary most by state or metro area?

Reference points:

- FRED’s downloadable FIXHAI table was opened and contains monthly observations through 2026; **verified by opening** ([FRED FIXHAI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/FIXHAI)).
- Census explains that vacancy rates are indicators of supply and demand; **not opened in this pass** ([Census ACS vacancy report](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2026/acs/acs-64.html)).
- Census HVS publishes vacancy and homeownership tables by state and metro area; **not opened in this pass** ([Census HVS](https://www.census.gov/housing/hvs/data/rates.html)).

Hard part: index definitions and geographic aggregation can create apparently contradictory stories.

Who can grade it: housing economist or demographer.

## 8. Can battery recycling materially reduce critical-mineral mining?

Find out how much battery recycling can contribute to lithium, nickel, and cobalt supply, when end-of-life material becomes large enough to matter, and how much the answer depends on collection, chemistry, and geography. Include a transparent percentage calculation rather than treating a headline potential as a forecast.

Subquestions: What are current battery and storage trends? What share of recycling feedstock is manufacturing scrap today and later? What demand reduction does IEA estimate by 2040? What changes by 2050? How do LFP batteries alter the mineral mix? Where is recycling capacity concentrated? Which assumptions are most uncertain?

Reference points:

- IEA’s 2025 minerals outlook says recycling can reduce mining needs 5–30% by 2040 and recycled minerals have roughly 80% lower GHG emissions than primary material; **verified by opening** ([IEA minerals outlook](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/overview-of-outlook-for-key-minerals)).
- IEA says manufacturing scrap is about two-thirds of recycling feedstock in 2030, with end-of-life material becoming dominant after 2040; **not opened in this pass** ([IEA recycling](https://www.iea.org/reports/recycling-of-critical-minerals/executive-summary)).
- IEA’s battery overview reports LFP at about 40% of EV sales and 80% of new storage in 2023; **not opened in this pass** ([IEA batteries](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary)).

Hard part: supply, demand, chemistry, collection, and processing capacity are separate denominators.

Who can grade it: battery-materials analyst or industrial ecologist.

## 9. What does methane mitigation progress actually show?

Find out whether recent methane-mitigation progress is large enough to bend global emissions, which sectors and countries account for the opportunity, and why satellite observations and national inventories can disagree. The answer should separate measured emissions, modeled estimates, and pledge tracking.

Subquestions: What does the 2025 Global Methane Status Report measure? Which sectors dominate? What targets are voluntary versus binding? How are satellites used? Why can inventories miss super-emitters? What is the difference between a percentage reduction and avoided warming? Which claims are robust across methods?

Reference points:

- UNEP’s Global Methane Status Report 2025 is the current global progress reference; **not opened in this pass** ([UNEP report](https://www.unep.org/resources/report/global-methane-status-report-2025)).
- The Global Methane Assessment explains methane’s climate role and mitigation framing; **not opened in this pass** ([UNEP assessment](https://wedocs.unep.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.11822/35917/GMA_ES.pdf)).

Hard part: inventories, satellite observations, and pledges answer different questions and use different baselines.

Who can grade it: atmospheric scientist or climate-policy analyst.

## 10. Does load growth threaten summer grid reliability?

Find out what the 2025 North American reliability assessment says about summer risk, how resource adequacy differs from transmission or fuel risk, and whether new large loads change the conclusion. Use the assessment’s regional tables and calculate at least one reserve or load comparison.

Subquestions: What planning horizon and weather assumptions does the assessment use? Which regions are highest risk? What resources are counted as firm? How do extreme heat and drought affect the result? Where do large loads appear in the assessment? Which mitigations are operational versus long-term construction?

Reference points:

- NERC’s 2025 Summer Reliability Assessment is a 54-page primary assessment; **verified by opening** ([NERC SRA 2025](https://www.nerc.com/globalassets/programs/rapa/ra/nerc_sra_2025.pdf)).
- NERC’s reliability-assessment resource page provides the surrounding assessment program; **not opened in this pass** ([NERC assessment resources](https://www.nerc.com/who-we-are/committees/reliability-and-security-technical-committee-rstc/subcommittees-working-groups-and-task-forces/reliability-assessment-subcommittee-ras/reliability-assessment-resources)).

Hard part: the report’s risk language is regional and conditional; “adequate” is not the same as “immune to outages.”

Who can grade it: reliability engineer or regional-transmission-organization planner.
